Most pages about college majors give you a ranking, a top ten, or somebody's opinion about which degree is worth it. This one is just the list.

It is built on the federal program taxonomy that colleges already use to classify what they teach, grouped into fields, with the common majors under each field. Skim the jump list, open the field you care about, and read down. Names vary by school, so treat each entry as a family of programs rather than a catalog line you can copy.

Two companion pages do the other jobs. If you want majors sorted by how common they actually are, with pay and trend data attached, read our [top 100 college majors ranked for 2026](/top-100-undergraduate-majors-2026/). If you want to know where a major tends to lead after graduation, read [college majors and careers](/college-majors-and-careers/). This page stays out of both arguments. It just lists what exists.

Where a major also appears in our 2026 ranking, the bullet says so and links across.

## How to read this list

The backbone is the Classification of Instructional Programs, the taxonomy the U.S. Department of Education uses to code what every American college teaches. The 2020 edition is the current one. Its top level is a set of two-digit series, which are the fields above, and each series contains four-digit and six-digit programs, which are the majors above. Fields are alphabetical here rather than in code order, and majors are alphabetical within each field.

We did not list every code. The taxonomy includes series that are not college majors in any ordinary sense: reserved series with no content, medical residency and fellowship series that come after a professional degree, high school credentials, basic skills and remedial instruction, citizenship and personal improvement courses, and professional military education. We also left out the trades series (construction, mechanic and repair, precision production, science technologies) because they are almost entirely certificate and associate training rather than bachelor's degrees. Where a field is mostly sub-baccalaureate but contains real bachelor's programs, we kept the field and listed only those programs. Aviation and engineering technology are the two clearest cases.

Names vary. The taxonomy says Registered Nursing and your school says Nursing. It says Sports, Kinesiology, and Physical Education and your school says Exercise Science. Where the official title is not what anyone actually calls the major, we used the common name and kept the meaning. Your registrar's catalog is the authority for your school, not this page or the federal list.

The ranking badges come from our own 2026 analysis of what majors people put on their resumes, which is a different question from what colleges award. A major can be common in the taxonomy and rare on a resume, or the reverse. Popularity, pay, and trend for each ranked major live in [the ranking itself](/top-100-undergraduate-majors-2026/).

## Questions people ask

### What is the difference between a major, a degree, and a concentration?

The degree is the credential: a Bachelor of Arts, a Bachelor of Science, a Bachelor of Fine Arts. The major is the subject you concentrated in to earn it. A concentration or track is a named path inside a major, so Finance inside a Business Administration major. Minors are smaller course clusters that appear on the transcript but do not define the degree. On paper, the degree and the major matter most. The concentration is worth naming only when it is the reason you are qualified.

### Can you double major, or add a minor?

At most schools, yes. A double major means completing the requirements of two majors under one degree, which usually works best when the two share general education and prerequisite courses. A dual degree means two separate degrees, often from two colleges within the same university, and takes longer. A minor is the cheapest option in credits and the weakest signal to an employer, which is fine when the point is your own interest rather than the resume.

### Can you change your major?

Most students can, and many do. The cost is measured in credits that no longer count toward the new requirements, which is why changing early is cheap and changing late is not. Competitive majors (nursing, engineering, some business programs) may require a separate application, a minimum grade point average, or a specific sequence of prerequisites, so check the requirements before you count on the switch.

### Where does your major go on a resume?

In the education section, as part of the degree line: the degree, the major, the school, and the graduation year. Do not spread it around. The exception is early career, when your coursework, labs, and projects are the strongest evidence you have. In that case the major stays in education, and the specific work moves into a projects or relevant coursework section where it can be read as experience rather than as a label. If you are not sure how your education section reads, put the document through our [free resume grader](https://ai.rezscore.com/) and look at the Presentation score.

### Why is my major not on this list?

Probably because your school named it something else, or because it sits one level below what we listed. The taxonomy has thousands of six-digit codes, and printing all of them would make this page unusable. If you cannot find yours, look for the field it belongs to and read the closest entry. If it is genuinely new, it may not be coded yet. The 2020 edition is a snapshot, and colleges invent programs faster than the classification updates.
